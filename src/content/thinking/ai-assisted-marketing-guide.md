This is general educational guidance. It does not describe an employer's technology, tools, data or working practices.

AI can help turn a clear brief into a draft. It cannot decide whether the brief serves the business, whether an offer is appropriate, or whether the material is yours to use.

## Start with the decision

Write down the audience, the customer need, the message and the action you want someone to take. A tool is easier to assess when the job is clear.

Use original material you own, public information that permits the intended use, or a fictional brief. Do not upload confidential documents, customer records, unpublished campaigns or access information.

## A hypothetical example

Imagine an independent sandwich shop planning a lunchtime poster. This is a fictional teaching example, not a client or employer case study.

The brief might be: explain the lunch offer clearly to people passing the shop. An AI assistant could suggest headline options from that brief. The owner would check the wording, confirm the offer and opening hours, review the image rights, and choose the final version.

No sales uplift or production-time saving is assumed. The example illustrates a way to think about the work, not a measured result.

## Give each draft a human review

Check these questions before publishing:

- Is every factual claim supported?
- Can a customer understand the offer and any limits?
- Are the words, images and other materials permitted for this use?
- Does the content avoid private information and implied endorsement?
- Is the final action clear and practical?

Generated code also needs review. A working prototype is a starting point for checking behaviour, access controls and data handling, rather than evidence that a system is ready for public use.

## Evaluate the tool on your own work

Use a small task with non-sensitive inputs. Compare the draft with your normal quality standard. Record the review effort as well as the drafting effort. Keep the tool only if the complete process is useful.
